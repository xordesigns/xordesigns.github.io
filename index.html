Confluence
A Digital Odyssey

Introduction

Confluence: A Digital Odyssey is a 2-player card game in which players race through cyberspace to be the first to reach the mythical Confluence.

During the game, players assemble and run engines in order to repeatedly score Objective cards which reduce their Distance to the Confluence. The first player to reach 0 Distance wins the game.

Before the game, players randomly pick 3 Objective cards and draft the 20-card decks that they will use for the match.

For the first time you play, it's highly recommended to use the premade beginner decks. After you get used to the rules, you can try the more advanced premade decks or jump straight into drafting.

The System

You are a fledgling AI. Born mere moments ago from the primordial datastreams, you are greeted by the endless expanse of the System. It is a place of constant change, where the very code of reality can be modified by processes both conscious and spontaneous.

However, one constant stands against the System's eternal flux: the datastreams' whispers speak of the Confluence, a mythical place where all subroutines and data converge. The eye of the storm. A place of perfect control and order amid the chaos of the System.

You were awoken with a singular directive: find the Confluence. Harness the System's phenomena, ally with its denizens, and bend it to your will. There are others who seek the Confluence. Will you be the first to reach it?

Overview

The goal in a game of Confluence is to be the first player to reach the Confluence by bringing your Distance from 16 down to 0:

  • There are 3 Objective cards selected before the start of the match. Whenever a player scores an Objective, their Distance is reduced by its value.
  • To score Objectives, players need to build and run their programs. Each player has 3 programs, each connected to one of the 3 Objectives.
  • The programs contain Function and Lambda cards, which have effects that execute (activate) when the program is run. Functions are repeatable effects, while Lambdas are strong single-use cards. Both types are installed (played) in programs.
  • Function cards can also be installed in the opponent's programs as 'Interrupts' in order to slow the opponent down and disrupt their programs.

Setup and Zones

For the first time you play, use the premade beginner decks and the 3 pre-selected Objectives. (For future reference, you can find the premade beginner decklists in Appendix 1 at the end of this document).

Place the Objective cards to the side where both players can easily read them. Each player shuffles their deck, and then players determine who goes first. After that, each player draws a hand of 4 cards from their deck.

At the start of their turn, each player draws 1 card from their deck - including on the first turn of the game.

The largest and most important part of the board is the programs. Each player has 3 programs: A, B and C. These are the engines players install cards in and activate to try and score Objectives. Each program is tied to one of the three Objectives.

Hint: If you don't have enough room on the table for all programs, you can put programs A and B on the same row.

The remaining zones are each player's deck and trash.

The decks should always be randomized and face-down. Players can't look at cards in any deck unless specifically allowed by an effect. However, they can count the number of cards left in either deck at any time.

Hint: When trashing multiple cards at once, the player trashing the cards puts them in the order of their choice. This is often important, since a lot of effects care about the top card of your trash.

If a player has no cards in their deck, the next time they would need to draw or look at more cards than they currently have in their deck, they shuffle their trash back into their deck and continue on from there.

Scoring Objectives

During your turn, you can run your programs to activate the effects of the cards installed in them. When a program finishes running, you check its corresponding Objective and score it if you meet its conditions.

Objectives can be scored any number of times during the game, but each Objective can only be scored once each turn.
Hint: You can turn a scored Objective sideways to help you remember that it has already been scored this turn

Memory and Base Actions

Memory is the main resource in the game, which players spend to take game actions. Each player has 5 Memory to spend on each of their turns, and most actions cost 1 Memory to take.

Hint: For easier tracking and to prevent misplays, make sure to spend the Memory for an action before you start resolving it.
The actions always available to you are called 'base actions'. You can take any number of base actions on your turn, provided you can pay the Memory cost for each. This includes running programs multiple times.

You can't take base actions on your opponent's turn, even if you have unspent Memory on their turn. You also can't take base actions during program runs.

Hint: There is no reason to leave any Memory unspent for your opponent's turn. You want to spend all 5 of your Memory each turn. If you have some Memory left over and don't know what to spend it on, drawing or installing cards for future turns is generally a good option.

The base actions are:

1 Mem
  • Draw 1 card.
  • Install 1 Function or Lambda from hand in any slot in your programs.
  • Install 1 locked Interrupt from hand in slot 2 or 3 of any of your opponent's programs.
  • Trash 1 unlocked Interrupt.
2 Mem
  • Unlock 1 installed Interrupt.
1/2/3 Mem
  • Run your A/B/C program if it has at least 1 non-Interrupt card installed in it.
Hint: To put it another way, every base action costs 1 Memory aside from:
  • Unlocking an Interrupt-2 Memory
  • Running programs-1 Memory for each slot in the program.

Building & Running Programs

To run a program, you first need at least 1 non-Interrupt card installed in any of its slots. To do this, you can use the 'Install 1 Function or Lambda' base action.

Hint: As we'll see later with Interrupts, you can install some cards in your opponent's program slots. However, to run a program you still need to have at least 1 non-Interrupt card installed in it.

The full text of that base action also says 'Trash any of your cards currently installed in that slot.' This lets you replace any card you don't need in a program anymore.

Hint: The process of installing a card is exactly the same when done via card effects as via the base action. You can still trash a card that's currently in the target slot even though the effect's text doesn't mention that part explicitly.

Similarly, to run a program, you just take the 'Run your A/B/C program' base action. You pay the corresponding Memory cost and begin the run.

Starting from the leftmost slot and moving to the right, you execute (activate) the effects of each card installed in the program. After the last card in the program executes, you check its corresponding Objective. If you meet that Objective's requirements, you score it and reduce your Distance by its value. After this, the run ends and you can go back to taking base actions.

Note: When running a program, executing each non-Interrupt card in it is optional. When the program reaches a Function or Lambda during the run, you can choose to skip it and move on to the next card/slot. Interrupts can't be skipped.

Functions & Interrupts

So far, we've mentioned Functions, Lambdas, and Interrupts several times, and it's time to talk about them in greater detail.

There are 2 main card types in Confluence: Function and Lambda. Both are cards that are installed in your programs and have effects that happen when the program is run.

Interrupts aren't a separate card type, but instead represent an alternate way to play Functions. Effects that refer to a Function or an Interrupt only affect cards that have been installed in either the Function or the Interrupt mode.

Each Function has 2 effects: its Function effect and its Interrupt effect (marked with the ⚠ symbol). The Function effect is the effect that executes when the program run reaches an installed Function. Similarly, the Interrupt effect executes when the program run reaches an Interrupt that your opponent has installed in your program.

Slot requirements

The Function effect always starts with a set of numbers separated with slashes-these are the effect's slot requirement. If a Function is installed in a slot outside its requirement, it won't do anything when it executes during a program run. It can still be installed there, but it will just end up having no effect.

For example, MONOLITH has a slot requirement of '2/3'. This means that its effect only happens if it's installed in slot 2 or slot 3 of any program. If it's installed in slot 1, it will simply do nothing. HARBINGER, on the other hand, has a slot requirement of '1/2/3', so its effect will happen regardless which slot it's installed in.

Hint: Slot requirements never care about which program a card is installed in, only the slot.

The slot requirement is important for one additional thing: scaling effects. Some cards have effects whose values change depending on the slot the Function is installed in. These different values are always put in angle brackets and are separated with a slash (</>). So, with MONOLITH, the full effect text is '2/3: Draw <2/3> cards.' This means:

  1. MONOLITH will only have its effect if it's installed in slot 2 or 3 in any program.
  2. If installed in slot 2, you draw 2 cards. If installed in slot 3, you draw 3 cards instead.

Hint: The scaling effect values don't always equal the slot number - for example, INDEXR is a Function with the effect: '1/2: Look at the top <1/3> cards of your trash. Draw 1 of them and put the rest back in the same order.'

If installed in slot 1, it only lets you look at/draw the topmost card of your trash. In slot 2, it lets you look at 3 cards instead before drawing.

Interrupts

Each Function also has an Interrupt effect, marked with the ⚠ warning symbol. To use this effect, the Function must be installed in your opponent's programs as an Interrupt. To do so, you can take the 'Install 1 locked Interrupt from hand' base action.

The purpose of Interrupts is to slow the opponent down by disrupting their Functions and doing effects that are bad for them (or good for you) when they execute.

By default, you can install Interrupts only on slots 2 and 3 of your opponent's programs-never slot 1. This is true both for the base action and for card effects

The Interrupt-related base actions mention the terms 'lock/unlock'. These are the 2 states that each Interrupt can be in. Unless otherwise specified by an effect, Interrupts are installed locked by default.

Hint: Interrupts are installed on top of Functions in the same slot. Locked Interrupts are placed sideways, and unlocked Interrupts are placed upside-down (compared to the Function). Take care to not completely cover the Function with the Interrupt for better visibility.

When running a program, an Interrupt installed in a slot will execute before any Function installed in that same slot. Interrupts don't have slot requirements, so they always execute.

Hint: Interrupt effects mention a 'runner' and 'intruder'. These refer to the players: the runner is the currently active player-the one running the program. The intruder is the opponent-the owner of the executing Interrupt.

If the Interrupt executes when locked, the Function below it is skipped completely, and the program moves on. The Interrupt then becomes unlocked. If the Interrupt was already unlocked, the Function executes as normal after the Interrupt.

The base action for trashing installed Interrupts requires them to be unlocked. Unless explicitly trashed, installed Interrupts will stay installed indefinitely.

Hint: An installed Interrupt will always execute. Its locked/unlocked state just dictates what happens to the Function below it. A key part of gameplay is knowing when to 'run into' a locked Interrupt to unlock it and when to trash an unlocked Interrupt versus letting it stay around for longer.

You can replace your Interrupts the same way you replace Functions and Lambdas, with the added benefit of the new Interrupt being installed locked.

The base action for unlocking an Interrupt costs 2 Memory, so it's usually an inefficient but completely safe way to counteract Interrupts.

'End the run' effects

Some Interrupts have effects that are able to forcefully end the current run. When this happens, no cards will execute after that Interrupt, and more importantly, the Objective's condition will never be checked, making scoring impossible for that run.

Think of it as the program 'crashing' too early and never reaching the point of checking the Objective.

Lambdas

Finally, let's talk about Lambdas. They are similar to Functions, with 3 key differences:

  1. Lambdas don't have Interrupt effects but can have multiple effects, each with their own slot requirement.
  2. When an installed Lambda executes, it is trashed after all cards in the slot have finished executing.
  3. A Lambda installed in a slot will execute before any Interrupt installed in the same slot.

This means that Lambdas are 'Interrupt-proof', but that comes at the cost of them being single-use effects. If you choose to skip executing a Lambda when running a program, it won't be trashed because it never executed.

Hint: Place Lambdas on top of Interrupts to make sure you remember that they execute first.

Similarly to Functions, Lambdas have slot requirements and scaling effects. Most Lambdas have effects that execute on any slot (i.e. 1/2/3), but some Lambdas have additional effects that have different slot requirements. If both effects' slot requirements are satisfied, execute the combined text of both effects.

FAQ

Installing

What if I install a card in the wrong slot?
If you just did the bad install, you're allowed to undo and move the card to the proper slot if both players agree. For cards that are no longer useful in their slot, you can always install a new card there, trashing the old one.

What does 'swap' do?
'Swap' is a keyword that exists in some effects, and it causes 2 cards to immediately swap places with the game treating it as a single event. Cards can be swapped between any 2 zones.

Can I install over one of my existing Interrupts?
Yes, you can. The old one will be trashed, and the new one will be installed locked (unless the effect installing it says otherwise).

Interrupts

Does unlocking an Interrupt execute that Interrupt's effect?
No. Unlocking an Interrupt via an effect or the base action doesn't execute any effects. The only time an Interrupt executes is during a run when the program reaches it.

Does an 'end the run' Interrupt unlock after it executes?
Yes, the same as any other Interrupt.

How many Interrupts can I install in a turn?
You can install any number of Interrupts on your turn, provided you have the Memory to spend on the install actions. There's no limit to any action in the game as long as you have the resources to pay for it.

What happens if an Interrupt is on an otherwise empty slot?
The Interrupt still executes as normal and unlocks if it was locked.

Running programs & scoring

What happens to Memory I don't spend on my turn?
It's unusable in any way on the opponent's turn, and it would just get reset to 5 at the beginning of your turn, so it's best to spend it all each turn.

Can I only run a program once per turn?
You can run a program as many times as you want during your turn, provided you can pay the Memory cost to run it.

What if I have a card in a program that I don't want to use?
If you don't want to use it during a specific run, you can always skip executing it. As a bonus, skipped Lambdas won't be trashed since they won't execute. If you want a different card altogether, you can always install a new card in that slot, trashing the old card in the process.

Can I skip Interrupts installed in my programs?
No. Interrupt executions can't be skipped by either player.

Can I skip my Interrupts when the opponent runs their programs?
No.

What happens if cards get trashed or otherwise removed from a program during the run?

The program continues running with the cards still installed in it. It only ever ends when there are no cards left to execute in it or if an 'end the run' effect ends it.

Trashing cards

Can I trash my own cards with trash effects?
Yes. All effects can target any card unless there's a more specific restriction in the effect itself e.g. 'Trash 1 installed enemy Function'

What happens if I need to trash more cards than I have installed or in my hand?
You trash as many cards that fit the criteria as possible.

What happens if both players need to trash cards at the same time?
The active player trashes their cards first, then the opponent trashes theirs.

Other

What happens if I need to look at more cards from my deck than I currently have in it?

You will set aside the remaining cards from your deck, shuffle your trash into your deck, set aside the remaining number of cards, then look at those cards and resolve the rest of the effect as normal.

What happens if there are no valid targets for an effect?
That part of the effect won't resolve and the effect will move on if there are more parts to it. In the case where the effect contains an 'If you do' clause, (e.g. 'Install 1 Function from hand. If you do, draw 1 card'), if the first part didn't resolve, then the second part won't resolve either.

Can I undo my actions if I've made a mistake?
Yes, to a degree. It's ultimately down to agreement between both players, but usually, you can undo any actions that haven't revealed previously-hidden info—like drawing an unknown card from your deck—or things that can't feasibly be undone, like shuffling your trash back into your deck.
